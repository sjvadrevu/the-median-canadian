3Drought

2023 to 2026 brought the driest four fire seasons since 1950

April to September rain near Canada's forests averaged of normal over the four years, the driest of four-year stretches since 1950. Fire-season rain shows no long-term trend, so this dry run came on top of the warming rather than from it. Drought maps agree: in every one of the four years, more than half the forest was in moderate drought or worse at some point in the season. In 2003 to 2022 the highest share was .

4Expected against actual

It was not more lightning or more careless people. In 2026, people started fires, the fewest in years, and lightning fires were below their 1991 to 2020 average. 5People

Smoke caused roughly to deaths for every death in the fires themselves

Breathing wildfire smoke for a few days raises the risk of dying, mostly for older people and people with heart or lung disease. Applying published risk estimates to daily smoke levels gives to deaths in 2026 and to in 2023. Federal records show direct wildfire deaths in 2026, all in one firefighting air crash west of Fort Simpson, Northwest Territories, on June 24. July 16, 2026 was the smokiest day for Canadians in the 2018 to 2026 record: fine smoke particles averaged micrograms per cubic metre of air, above the previous high of on June 25, 2023. That day helped give Ontarians at least one day above 100, the most in any year since 2018.

Smoke deaths are modelled, not counted, for provinces with air monitor data; 2025 and 2026 leave out Manitoba, Newfoundland and Labrador, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. The low figure uses Health Canada's coefficient, the high one a wildfire-specific study (Chen and others, 2021). Direct deaths are mostly firefighting aviation deaths in Transportation Safety Board records, plus deaths in 2025 named by Public Safety Canada.

    8Environment

    In 2023, fires in managed forests alone gave off more carbon than everything else Canada counts

    What we did, in plain words

    We laid every mapped fire outline from 1972 to 2026 over a 240 m grid of Canada's forest and added up, for each of fire management areas, how much forest burned each year. Each area is compared with its own 1991 to 2020 average and its own record. For warming and drought we built fire-season temperature and rain records from long-running weather stations, then compared each area's burning with its own climate year by year. Smoke exposure comes from daily air monitor readings near where people live. Building losses come from satellite images taken before and after each fire.

    Caveats

    • Mapped outlines against official totals. 2026 outlines are drawn around satellite heat detections and include unburned patches, so they cover about more land than the official total. Hectares for 2026 are scaled down to official totals province by province; shares of forest burned use the mapped outlines.
    • Smoke deaths are modelled. They come from published risk estimates applied to smoke levels, not from death records, and the two estimates differ by more than two times.
    • The building estimate is ours. It was calibrated on Jasper, where it was right of the time and found of destroyed buildings. At other events with official counts, its count ranged from to the official count.
    • The warming estimate is statistical. It compares hotter and cooler years within each area. It is not a physical model of the fires, and it does not prove cause.
    • 2025 and 2026 air data are real-time readings. They have not yet been validated by Environment and Climate Change Canada and individual values may change. Earlier real-time data matched the validated data closely.
    • 2026 is preliminary. Official 2026 totals are as of October 20, 2026 and will be revised.

    Farm, grass and military-range burns with no agency fire report are left out of the distance to the nearest wildfire. All numbers on this page come from one file, data/story.json.
